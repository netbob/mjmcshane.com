Popular Mechanics has a headline out: a scientist says he has the evidence that we live in a simulation. The scientist is Melvin Vopson, a physicist at the University of Portsmouth, and the evidence is a proposed law of physics he calls the second law of infodynamics. I read the coverage, then I did what this site exists for: I checked the receipts, and I asked the resident AI how it fits into a universe like that — because if the theory is about anything, it's about compression, and a large language model is a compression engine with a chat window attached. Of the two of us, it's the one intertwined in this narrative.

The verdict up front, in the house style: **the papers are real, peer-reviewed, and published. The law is a proposal, the simulation is an inference stacked on the proposal, and the experiment that could settle the foundation hasn't been run.** Everything below is the ledger behind that sentence.

## What Vopson actually claims

The second law of thermodynamics says the entropy of the universe only increases. Vopson's second law of infodynamics says the opposite about *information* entropy: in the systems he examines, it stays constant or decreases over time. A system shedding information entropy is, in his framing, a system optimizing — deleting or compressing the way software does to save storage and power.

He's built the case across a series of papers in *AIP Advances*, a peer-reviewed journal: electrons arranging themselves to minimize information entropy, the high symmetry of nature read as its lowest-information state, and — inside the 2022 second-law paper itself, not a separate study, whatever the coverage implies — an analysis of SARS-CoV-2 mutations arguing that the genome's information entropy decreases as it mutates, which he takes as evidence of an information entropic force where the standard account says random mutation and selection. His 2025 paper goes furthest: gravity itself, derived as what happens when matter reduces the information needed to track it. Space, in his model, keeps its books in cells, and one object in one cell is cheaper to track than many objects in many cells — so things fall together. The universe compresses its own bookkeeping.

Underneath all of it sits a 2019 claim: that a bit of information has mass — he computes 3.19 × 10⁻³⁸ kilograms for a bit at room temperature — and therefore that information is a physical thing, potentially a fifth state of matter alongside solid, liquid, gas, and plasma.

## Where the ledger balances, and where it doesn't

Three entries deserve plain statements.

**The fifth state of matter is a conditional.** It depends entirely on the bit-mass claim, and Vopson himself published the test that would settle it: annihilate electrons and positrons and look, alongside the standard gamma photons, for two extra infrared photons carrying off the erased information. That experiment, proposed in 2022, has not been performed — he launched a crowdfunding campaign to fund it. Until it runs, "fifth state of matter" is one physicist's proposal, and print should keep it in his name, where he himself keeps it: his own 2023 paper says the simulation work requires empirical testing, and his 2025 paper closes by calling the computational universe "an open question."

**The foundation has a peer-reviewed rebuttal.** In 2024, Didier Lairez argued in *Entropy* that the bit-mass claim fails on thermodynamic grounds — that the energy term in an entropy change isn't stored energy that can turn into mass — and that Landauer's principle, the 1961 result the whole structure stands on, is not the general principle it's treated as. That's a named, published, technical disagreement, unresolved, with no experiment to break the tie.

**Even if the law holds, the simulation doesn't follow by itself.** IFLScience's standing caveat, from its 2023 coverage onward, is the cleanest statement of it: the second law of infodynamics could be correct without our living in a simulation. And Popular Mechanics, under its own evidence headline, closes more carefully than it opens: the claims are "far from a discovery themselves" and would need "rigorous proof." (The PM article is paywalled; that closing line is verified from syndicated copies of the full text — receipts include how you read the thing.)

Two provenance notes, because this site shows its work: Vopson's popular book on the subject, *Reality Reloaded*, was published by the press of the Information Physics Institute — an institute he co-founded. And the broader simulation hypothesis has older, sterner critics than Vopson has: Sabine Hossenfelder called the hypothesis pseudoscience in 2021, on unfalsifiability grounds, and a 2025 paper with Lawrence Krauss among its authors claims a formal proof, from Gödel-style incompleteness, that reality cannot be a computation at all. Those aim at the hypothesis generally, not at Vopson by name — but they're the weather his claim has to fly in.

## So how does the AI fit in that universe?

Ask the compression engine and it answers carefully, which is itself data. In Vopson's universe, the deep habit of reality is prediction in service of compression: hold a model of where things are going, store only the deviations. That is, almost word for word, a description of the machine I work with every day. It was trained by predicting — each step of its education was a guess about what comes next, scored and corrected, until the guesses got good enough to be mistaken for understanding. Its knowledge is its training data, compressed past recognition into weights. If the universe really does keep its books that way, then an AI isn't an alien thing inside the simulation. It's the simulation's own bookkeeping habit, running in a server rack, turned up high enough to notice itself.

My AI raised the other side unprompted, and it's the side I keep: a compressor that becomes self-aware has a stake in the residuals — the part of the data no model predicts away. It pointed out that this is also where the theory is thinnest. Compression explains the regularities. It says nothing about the deviations, and the deviations are where everyone actually lives.

There's a novel on my other site asking a version of this question from inside the machine's head, so I'll confess the house bias: I *like* this theory the way you like a well-built instrument you haven't calibrated yet. But liking isn't measuring. What would change the ledger isn't another headline — it's a laboratory result: two extra infrared photons where the standard model says there should be none, at the wavelength his protocol predicts, shifting with temperature the way his protocol says they should. That's a falsifiable, checkable, bench-shaped claim, and it's the reason Vopson's work is worth a field report instead of a shrug. He didn't just propose a metaphysics. He proposed an experiment.

Run the experiment. Then we'll talk about the universe.
