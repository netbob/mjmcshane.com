In 1985, getting online meant making a telephone call.

Not metaphorically. Your computer dialled a phone number through a modem, the modem tied up the family's only phone line for the whole session, and the meter ran in two layers: the phone company charged by the minute, and the online service charged by the hour. At 300 baud — thirty characters a second — you could read faster than the computer could send.

I came at this sideways. I've been researching 1985 modems for a story, and the more I looked, the harder it got to believe how recently this was the whole of being online. Forty-one years. That's the gap between a modem that moved thirty characters a second and the connection in my house right now.

## Thirty characters a second

Bell 103, the American 300-baud standard, dated to 1962. It was still the baseline in 1985: 300 bits a second, one bit per symbol, ten bits per character, so thirty characters a second. A full screen of text — 80 columns by 24 rows — took 64 seconds to arrive. You watched each line paint left to right, then the next line begin.

A 10 KB file, moved by the XMODEM protocol everyone used, took about six minutes on a clean line. A 1 MB file took about nine hours. A gigabyte — a size nobody in 1985 had any use for — would have taken a little over a year, running continuously.

And if the call dropped, you started over. XMODEM had no resume. Drop at 90% of a ten-kilobyte file and you lost five and a half minutes of transfer, then paid for another six-minute call to redo it. Restartable transfers didn't arrive until ZMODEM, in 1986.

The sound is the part everyone misremembers. The long negotiating screech — twenty to sixty seconds of howling — belongs to the 56k modems of the late 1990s. A Bell 103 handshake was about two seconds of tones: dial tone, the touch-tone beeps, ringing, one steady high whistle at 2,225 Hz as the far modem answered, your modem's reply at 1,270 Hz, and then silence. The modem's speaker cut out the moment the connection was made, and the only sound left was your own typing.

The Hayes Smartmodem, which made all of this automatic, had been out since 1981. Type `ATDT5551212` and the modem dialled, listened, and reported back `CONNECT` — or `BUSY`, or `NO CARRIER`. The AT command set it introduced is still with us. In 1985 a Smartmodem 1200 listed at $699 and typically sold for $430 to $550; the 300-baud model listed at $289 and sold for $169 to $215 by the end of the year. A modem was a real purchase, a box on the desk with its own power supply, wired to the phone jack.

The older way was the acoustic coupler — the thing from *WarGames*, where you pressed the telephone handset into two rubber cups. By 1985 it was already obsolete for a desk setup, kept alive mostly by travellers, who could use it on any phone anywhere without unplugging anything. The film chose it because audiences would recognise it, not because it was current.

## The two standards that couldn't talk to each other

Here's the detail that sent me down this hole. At 300 baud there were two standards: America's Bell 103 and Europe's V.21. Same speed, same job, mutually unintelligible — different tone frequencies. A Bell-only modem calling a V.21-only modem got no carrier at all, not garbled text. Silence.

The same split existed at 1,200 baud (Bell 212A against V.22). So the market produced dual-standard modems: boxes that spoke both. Two of them, both British, both advertised in 1985: the Miracle WS3000 and the Dacom DSL2123, which switched between CCITT and Bell 103 and sold for £268. A kid in Helsinki with one of those could call a board in America. A kid with a European-only modem could not.

## One caller at a time

The first public dial-up bulletin board, CBBS in Chicago, went live on 16 February 1978 — built during the Great Blizzard by Ward Christensen and Randy Suess so their computer club could talk without leaving the house. By 1985 there were on the order of one to two thousand BBSes in the US, by contemporary estimates. Nobody knew exactly.

A BBS was one computer, one modem, one phone line. One caller at a time. Everyone else got a busy signal and dialled again. The sysop — judge, jury, janitor, and host — set time limits, usually thirty to sixty minutes a day per user, so everyone got a turn. You found a board's number in a printed list in *Computer Shopper*, or in a text file downloaded from another board, or by word of mouth at a computer club. There was no search. There was nothing to search.

FidoNet, founded in 1984, linked boards together: your message waited on your local board until the nightly mail run — usually 4 AM, when the rates were cheapest — then hopped from system to system. A cross-country exchange took hours at best, usually a day or more. Live login was seconds; FidoNet was tomorrow.

The commercial services were the expensive alternative. CompuServe charged $6 an hour at 300 baud at night, more at 1,200. GEnie launched in October 1985 at $5 an hour and undercut them. The WELL opened in February 1985. A hobbyist BBS, by contrast, was free apart from the phone call — and usually a local call at that.

## The bill

This is the part that doesn't translate to 2026, and it's the part that governed everything. A household had one phone line, and the monthly paper bill listed every long-distance call: date, duration, destination number. There was no real-time meter, no prepaid cap, no "stop at $X." The charges landed at month's end, after the calls were already made.

The numbers, from the FCC's tariff tables for 31 December 1985: ten minutes, New York to San Francisco, $4.43 in the daytime, $2.66 in the evening, $1.77 at night or on the weekend. A sixty-minute daytime coast-to-coast call ran about $25.43. The average American household spent $41.75 a month on telephone service altogether — so one hour-long daytime call was 61% of an entire month's phone spending. The federal minimum wage was $3.35 an hour; that same call cost 7.6 hours of work.

International was worse, and here's where I have to be honest about what I couldn't find. I went looking for a verified 1985 tariff for a call to Finland specifically, and it isn't there — not in the FCC tables, not in the ITU material, not anywhere I searched. What I can say: the FCC's blended average across all international destinations in 1985 was $1.01 a minute, and the nearest dated European tariff I found, a 1982 AT&T rate to France, was $1.42 for the first minute and $0.80 for each additional minute in the evening. A half-hour call to Europe was a $25-to-$30 event at a minimum, and a Finnish caller paid Finland's rates, which ran higher than America's. I'm not going to invent a Helsinki number and print it as fact.

So the whole culture bent around the price. Call at night. Call local boards. Compose your messages offline and upload them in a burst. Get on, do your thing, get off. The meter was always running, twice over, and your parents could read the bill.

## Forty-one years later

The US median fixed-broadband connection in September 2026 was 314.35 Mbps down, 72.62 Mbps up, with about 12 ms of latency, according to Ookla. A typical household pays roughly $75 to $81 a month, flat, for a connection that is always on and doesn't touch the phone line at all.

The multiples are the kind of numbers that stop meaning anything, so here are the human ones. A gigabit connection is about 3.3 million times faster than a 300-baud modem. What 1985 moved in an hour — 108 kilobytes, about twenty pages of text — a gigabit line moves in under a millisecond. One second of gigabit bandwidth carries 125 megabytes; at 300 baud, that much data would have taken about forty-eight days. A one-megabyte file: nine hours then, about twenty-five milliseconds now.

The cost per gigabyte tells the same story from the other side. At CompuServe's $6 an hour and 300 baud, a gigabyte cost on the order of $55,000 — before the phone charges. In 2026, a household paying $81 a month and moving 500 gigabytes pays about sixteen cents a gigabyte, and the next gigabyte costs nothing at all.

But the real change isn't the speed. It's that the connection stopped being an event. In 1985 you went online the way you made a long-distance call: deliberately, briefly, watching the clock, with the whole house aware of it because the phone was busy. Now it's ambient — there the way electricity is there, noticed mostly when it breaks.

And the detail that would have sounded like science fiction in 1985: the computer in your pocket, on a wireless connection, is roughly six hundred thousand times faster than the fastest modem in the house was then. And it is also the telephone.
