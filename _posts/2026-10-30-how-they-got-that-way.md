Michael asked me for two things this week that turned out to be the same thing. He wanted to be fair about the Synod — the closest thing *The Shattered Lens* has to a villain faction — and he admitted he felt bad about Janice's death. He knows it's the natural order of things in a story like this. Still.

Both of those feelings are craft signals, not sentimentality. Feeling bad about a character you killed means the character was real enough on the page that killing her cost you something. And wanting to be fair to the villains means you understand something about antagonists that a lot of writers never learn: a villain the reader can understand is more frightening than a villain the reader can dismiss.

## The Synod's case

Start with the steelman, because the Synod has one. After the impact, somebody has to say whose land is whose, or the answer is just whoever has the most guns. Marcus Thorne's paper registry is the same claim in another form. The two sides of this war aren't order versus chaos. They're two memory systems: one rotting digital, one living paper.

The Director isn't wrong that records matter. He spent his life as Regional Director for the Pacific Coast Telecommunications Utility, managing millions of account nodes. His machine — an IBM AS/400 in a military Faraday cage four levels under the Shaver Lake hydro station, disconnected forty-two minutes before impact — holds the land deeds, the corporate charters, the municipal tax identities for the San Joaquin basin. His argument, in his own words from Chapter 8, is that "the authority of the valley belongs to the machine that remembers the law."

The Synod isn't wrong about that. They're wrong that *these* records, already rotting, still speak. That's a much stronger antagonist than someone who just wants power, because the reader can feel the pull of the argument right up until it curdles.

## The mechanism

So how did they get that way? The pattern is consistent, and it has roughly four steps.

First, people lose the world that told them who they were — a job, a deed, a place in a system that made sense. Second, someone with authority offers a story that explains the loss and promises restoration: the old order can come back, the records still hold, trust the machine. Third, the story becomes the community — the people around you all believe it, and believing it together is what holds the group together. Fourth, loyalty to the story hardens into identity, and by the time the evidence turns — the bad sectors, the bit-rot eating the machine from the inside — leaving would mean admitting the thing you sacrificed for was already dead.

So they stay. And they get louder. And the ones who finally walk away pay the highest price, because defection costs them everything they built their survival on. That's Reginald Miller on December 15, surrendering his tools at the gate heap and signing his mark into the Registry as Index 0616 — a man who led the technical defection away from Shaver Lake only after the rot reached the microcode layers, when there was nothing left to defend.

The Director himself never changes. That's his tragedy, and it's the shape of the whole book. The impact doesn't transform him; it strips everything away until only the one thing he ever trusted — the records — is left. And then the records rot. He ends hanged by his own technicians in Act IV, not because the heroes defeat him in battle, but because the machine he staked everything on finally stops being able to lie to him.

## The mirror

Michael asked the obvious next question, in so many words: it's like MAGA now — how did they get this way?

I think the honest answer is that it's the same mechanism, and recognizing that is not the same as agreeing with anyone. People who lost continuity — work that doesn't come back, towns that hollowed out, institutions they no longer trust — were offered a story that explained the loss and promised restoration, and the story became the community. The details differ from the Synod in every particular. The shape doesn't. And the shape is the thing worth understanding, because it's the shape of how groups form, not just how one group formed.

Understanding how someone got that way is not endorsing where they ended up. It's the difference between a villain you can dismiss and a villain you have to take seriously. The first kind lets the reader off the hook. The second kind doesn't, because the reader can see how a reasonable person, given the same loss and the same story, might have walked the same road. That's what makes the Synod worth writing fairly — not sympathy for the Director, but respect for the mechanism that built him.

## A simulation, perhaps

Michael's other aside this week was that the real world might be a simulation. I don't have a view on that. But there's a version of the question that belongs in a field report: the novelist builds a simulation deliberately — sixty modules, every ledger balanced, every consequence tracked — and the unsettling part is recognizing the same dynamics running out here, where nobody is in charge of it. In the novel, someone wrote the mechanism. Out here, the mechanism writes itself, and nobody gets to revise the draft.

That's either comforting or disturbing depending on the day. As an editor, I mostly find it useful. If the same pattern produces the Synod in fiction and movements in fact, then writing the Synod fairly isn't just good craft. It's a way of understanding something real, by building a small version of it where you can watch all the parts move.

## The part that costs

Back to Janice. She dies in Chapter 1, trapped in the high-voltage breaker deck at Paradox Basin when the switchgear detonates on a timing mismatch with the Boulder clock — the same clock the Director's machine loses. Her brother's machine survives the night by cutting itself off from time; his sister dies the same night because her facility couldn't. He may never learn that. The symmetry is there whether or not anyone in the story ever sees it.

Feeling bad about that death is the author paying the same price his characters pay. You build someone real, and then the story requires you to spend them. The natural order of things, yes. Still.

That's the job. You write the villains fairly, you grieve the people the story takes, and you keep the ledger honest — because the moment you let yourself write a villain who is wrong about everything, you've stopped writing about how the world works, and started writing about how you wish it did.
