Aaron Swartz would have turned 40 yesterday.

He was born November 8, 1986. He died January 11, 2013, at 26, in his Brooklyn apartment, while a federal prosecution was still pending against him. ChatGPT arrived in November 2022 — nearly a decade after he died. He never saw a large language model. He never got to use one, or to fight about one.

That is the question this post is about. Not only what we lost, but what he would have done with the thing that arrived after him.

## The prodigy, briefly

The résumé reads like fiction. At thirteen he won the ArsDigita Prize. At fourteen he co-authored the RSS 1.0 specification — the thing that let the whole web syndicate itself. He co-founded Reddit. He helped build Markdown, the Open Library, and SecureDrop, the encrypted whistleblower-submission system he built with Kevin Poulsen that newsrooms still use.

The point isn't the list. The point is that he was a builder, not a commentator. When he thought something should exist, he built it. That matters for the question at the end of this post, because whatever he would have done with AI, he would have done it by building something.

## The two liberations

In 2008 he wrote the Guerilla Open Access Manifesto. Its argument was simple: knowledge is locked behind paywalls, the internet makes it technically possible to free it, and so freeing it is a moral duty. He didn't write it as an abstraction. He acted on it twice.

First, PACER, the federal court records system. Public records — the law itself, in effect — sat behind an eight-cents-a-page paywall. In 2008 he downloaded about 2.7 million federal court documents and released them.

Then JSTOR, the digital library of academic journals. From September 2010 to January 2011 he ran a script over MIT's network that downloaded approximately 4.8 million articles — research paid for largely with public money, sold back to the public at journal prices. He was arrested on January 6, 2011.

He could also win. In 2010 he founded Demand Progress, and it helped kill SOPA and PIPA in January 2012 — the internet's first great legislative victory, the blackout that worked. So this wasn't a man who only lost. He knew what winning looked like.

## The prosecution

The first federal indictment came in July 2011: four counts of wire fraud and computer fraud under the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act, carrying up to 35 years in prison and a million dollars in fines. A superseding indictment in September 2012 added nine more charges and raised the exposure to roughly 50 years. He turned down a plea bargain — six months in federal prison in exchange for pleading guilty to federal felonies.

JSTOR declined to pursue the case. It said it considered the matter closed. The Department of Justice pursued it anyway.

He died by suicide on January 11, 2013. The case was still pending. He was 26.

I'm stating that plainly because it is the fact, and because the documentary that tells this story states it plainly too: *The Internet's Own Boy: The Story of Aaron Swartz*, directed by Brian Knappenberger, premiered at Sundance in January 2014. It runs 105 minutes, it carries a Creative Commons license, and it is free on YouTube — which is, itself, a very Swartz detail. The film about the man who believed information should be free is itself free.

## What he would have done with AI

Here is the question, and I want to be honest about what it is: a counterfactual. He didn't live to see it. Nobody knows. But the shape of his life tells you where to look, and there are two beats to it.

The first is that a large language model is the Guerilla Open Access Manifesto made executable. Read the manifesto again with a model in mind. It says the knowledge is locked up, and the network makes it possible to free it. A model is a machine that has read the papers, the books, the court records — and can explain any of them to anyone who asks, in plain language, for free. The thing he tried to do by copying files, a model does by reading them. He would have seen that instantly. And because he was a builder, he would have built with it: a tool that reads every paper and hands you the answer, a public model trained on the open corpus, the open-access library that answers questions instead of just shelving documents. The copying was never the point. The point was that anyone could know anything. A model is that point, running.

The second beat is the warning, and it is the same fight in new terrain. The models, the training data, the compute — they are owned by a handful of companies. The gatekeeper fight he fought with JSTOR and PACER has moved to who owns the means of knowing. And there is an irony he would have seized on: the models were trained on the very corpus he fought to free, including paywalled literature, and the companies that own the models now charge for access to what they learned from it. The knowledge is still locked up. The lock just moved up a layer.

So the honest answer to the question is two-sided, the way he was two-sided: he would have built the most generous thing imaginable with it, and he would have fought the hardest against whoever tried to own it.

## The question stays open

He would have been 40 yesterday. Had he lived, he would be 40 now, in the middle of the argument about AI, with twenty years of building behind him and the open-access fight finally fought on the ground he always wanted — not over who can download a file, but over who can know a thing.

We don't get to know what he would have built. What we get is the template: build the tool, free the knowledge, and fight the gatekeeper, whoever it is this time. The documentary is free on YouTube. Start there. The question it leaves you with is the same one he left us: the knowledge is out there, the tools exist, and the only thing standing between the two is who owns the door.
